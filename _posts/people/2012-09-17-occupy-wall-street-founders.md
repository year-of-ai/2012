**Key figures**: Kalle Lasn (Adbusters founder, born 1942), Micah White (Adbusters editor and co-creator of the OWS call to action, born 1985), David Graeber (anthropologist and anarchist theorist, 1961–2020), the Direct Action Network (horizontal organizing collective), Anonymous and hacktivist networks (digital organizers), and Naomi Klein (public intellectual and early OWS spokesperson).

## Background

The Occupy Wall Street movement emerged at the intersection of post-2008 financial crisis discontent, the Arab Spring's demonstration of mass street protest, and decades of activism against neoliberal economic policy. While the movement is dated to **September 17, 2011** — the date of the initial occupation of Zuccotti Park (formerly Liberty Plaza Park, which protesters renamed "Liberty Square") in lower Manhattan, New York City — its intellectual and organizational origins trace to the ideological commitments of specific activist networks with roots going back to the 1990s antiglobalization movement.

The genesis narrative centers on **Adbusters**, a Vancouver-based magazine and activist collective founded in 1989 by **Kalle Lasn**. Adbusters had spent two decades advancing critiques of consumerism and corporate media through culture jamming and provocative print campaigns. On **July 13, 2011**, Adbusters posted a single-image call to action online: a silhouetted woman in a yoga pose atop a bull statue (referencing Wall Street's Charging Bull sculpture), with the text "#OccupyWallStreet" and a proposed action date of September 17, 2011. The post circulated on Reddit, Tumblr, and Twitter, attracting coordination from previously disconnected activist communities.

By September 17, 2011, approximately **1,000 demonstrators** gathered in lower Manhattan; roughly 200 remained overnight to begin the occupation of Zuccotti Park. Within two weeks, sister occupations had spread to over 100 U.S. cities. By October 15, 2011 — the movement's first "global day of action" — coordinated demonstrations occurred in **951 cities across 82 countries**, according to organizer estimates.

## Key Ideological Figures

### Kalle Lasn and Adbusters

Kalle Lasn (born October 24, 1942, in Tallinn, Estonia) fled Soviet occupation with his family as a child and arrived in Canada in 1951. He later studied economics at the Australian National University and worked in advertising filmmaking in Japan and Canada before founding Adbusters Media Foundation in Vancouver in 1989. Adbusters' founding mission was to challenge corporate colonization of public space and consciousness through the vehicle of "subvertising" — appropriating advertising aesthetics to deliver anti-consumerist messages.

Lasn's intellectual project synthesized critiques of consumerism drawn from theorists **Guy Debord** (*The Society of the Spectacle*, 1967), **Jean Baudrillard** (*Simulacra and Simulation*, 1981), and Marshall McLuhan's media theory. His 1999 book *Culture Jam: The Uncooling of America* articulated a philosophy of cultural resistance as a form of political action, arguing that changing consciousness was prerequisite to changing political economy.

By 2012, Lasn occupied the role of the movement's elder statesman, appearing frequently in media interviews and positioning OWS as a response to unresolved post-2008 economic inequality. He maintained that the movement's most important achievement was not specific policy wins but the introduction of "economic inequality" as a permanent feature of mainstream political discourse — specifically the "1% vs. 99%" framing that subsequent political campaigns (including Bernie Sanders' 2016 and 2020 presidential campaigns) adopted as core vocabulary.

### Micah White

Micah White (born September 28, 1985, in Virginia) joined Adbusters in the late 2000s as a senior editor after completing his undergraduate education at Swarthmore College and beginning graduate work. White co-authored the foundational Adbusters call to action with Lasn and was the primary architect of the #OccupyWallStreet hashtag strategy — one of the earliest large-scale uses of a hashtag to coordinate real-world political action rather than online commentary.

White's approach to activism drew on participatory media theory, Guy Debord's situationism, and skepticism of hierarchical organizations. Writing in Adbusters and later in his 2016 book *The End of Protest: A New Playbook for Revolution*, White argued that OWS demonstrated both the unprecedented power and fundamental limitations of decentralized protest: the horizontal model could aggregate hundreds of thousands of participants globally but struggled to translate that energy into durable institutional change.

White later became openly critical of the movement he helped spark, arguing in interviews that OWS "failed to achieve its goals" and that future activism required either abandoning street protest for electoral engagement or developing new forms of power that could outlast initial surges of mobilization.

### David Graeber

**David Graeber** (February 12, 1961 – September 2, 2020) was an American anthropologist and anarchist political theorist whose influence on OWS was both intellectual and organizational. Graeber held a faculty position at Yale University until 2007, when his contract was not renewed — a decision Graeber and supporters attributed to retaliation for his union organizing and political activism. He subsequently held a position at Goldsmiths, University of London, from 2008 until his death.

Graeber's 2011 book *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* — published by Melville House on July 12, 2011, just weeks before the Adbusters call appeared — provided the historical scaffolding for OWS's economic critique. Drawing on five thousand years of cross-cultural economic history, Graeber argued that debt was not a neutral financial instrument but a historically specific tool of social control, and that the moral authority societies grant to creditors over debtors is constructed, not natural. The book sold over a million copies globally and was widely cited by OWS participants as formative reading.

Graeber was among the early organizers of the September 17 action and is credited with helping introduce **horizontal consensus decision-making through general assemblies** — a democratic process derived from anarchist organizing tradition, in which facilitators use hand signals to indicate agreement, disagreement, or the desire to speak, without voting in the majoritarian sense. The **"people's microphone"** (also called the "human microphone") — in which a crowd relays a speaker's words back in unison, amplifying voice without electronic amplification — was developed by Graeber and other organizers in the early days of Zuccotti Park when electronic amplification was prohibited.

In 2013, Graeber published *The Democracy Project: A History, a Crisis, a Movement*, a direct account of his role in organizing OWS and an analysis of the movement's strengths and limitations. He died on September 2, 2020, while vacationing in Venice, at the age of 59; the cause of death was later identified as necrotizing pancreatitis.

### Naomi Klein

**Naomi Klein** (born May 8, 1970), the Canadian author and social activist best known for *No Logo* (2000) and *The Shock Doctrine* (2007), emerged as an early public spokesperson for OWS without having been a formal organizer. Klein visited Zuccotti Park in early October 2011 and delivered a widely circulated speech — later published in *The Nation* — articulating the movement's significance in systemic terms. Her framing of OWS as a response to "disaster capitalism" and the long-term consequences of the 2008 financial bailouts provided a ready-made narrative for journalists and commentators seeking to contextualize the protests.

### The Direct Action Network and Horizontal Structure

OWS deliberately rejected formal leadership structures, inspired by anarchist and horizontalist principles developed through decades of activist organizing in Latin America (particularly the Argentinian *asambleas barriales* movement of 2001) and indigenous rights activism (the Zapatista model of "mandar obedeciendo" — ruling by obeying). Key organizing formations included:

- **New York City General Assembly (NYCGA)** — the primary decision-making body, meeting nightly in Zuccotti Park using consensus-based process
- **Facilitation Working Group** — trained facilitators who ran general assemblies
- **Legal Working Group** — coordinated with the National Lawyers Guild to provide legal support for arrested protestors; provided over **1,600 legal observer volunteers** during the movement's peak
- **Media Working Group** — maintained the OccupyWallSt.org website and coordinated with press
- **Direct Action Working Group** — planned civil disobedience and coordinated with other cities
- **Hacktivist networks** (aligned loosely with "Anonymous") — managed digital security and livestreaming infrastructure

The New York Police Department ultimately **arrested over 700 demonstrators** on the Brooklyn Bridge on October 1, 2011 — one of the largest mass arrests in New York City history — a confrontation that generated substantial media coverage and boosted the movement's national profile.

## 2012: The Movement's Second Year

### May Day General Strike

The movement's most ambitious 2012 action was the **May 1, 2012 General Strike** — coordinated with labor unions, immigrant rights organizations, and student groups across the United States and internationally. The action, framed as the "first general strike in the United States since 1946," involved demonstrations in over **115 U.S. cities** and coordinated actions in dozens of international cities. In Oakland, California, where Occupy Oakland had been among the most militant local chapters (the Oakland general strike of November 2, 2011 had briefly shut down the Port of Oakland), thousands marched and confronted police.

The May Day action demonstrated OWS's capacity to coordinate with established labor organizations — a relationship that had been tense during the initial occupation phase, when some unions were cautious about aligning with an anarchist-inflected movement.

### Occupy Sandy

In the weeks following Hurricane Sandy's landfall in New York City on **October 29, 2012**, OWS activists pivoted to mutual-aid disaster relief, organizing under the name **Occupy Sandy**. Volunteers established distribution hubs in Brooklyn and Queens churches and community centers, coordinating food, clothing, and supplies for tens of thousands of residents in affected neighborhoods — Coney Island, Red Hook, the Rockaways — that government relief agencies were slow to reach.

The Occupy Sandy effort organized approximately **60,000 volunteers** through a network of hubs across the city and distributed an estimated **$1.2 million in supplies** in the months following the storm. It became widely cited in academic and policy literature as a model of decentralized mutual aid — the same horizontal organizing principles that had governed Zuccotti Park applied to disaster relief logistics.

### Strike Debt and Rolling Jubilee

On **November 15, 2012** — the one-year anniversary of the police clearing of Zuccotti Park — OWS activists launched **Strike Debt**, a campaign focused specifically on the politics of personal debt: student loans, medical debt, and housing debt. The campaign's first project, **Rolling Jubilee**, was a crowd-funded initiative that purchased distressed consumer debt at pennies on the dollar from debt collectors and abolished it rather than collecting — demonstrating the mechanics of the secondary debt market to a mass audience. By 2014, Rolling Jubilee had raised $701,317 in donations and used those funds to abolish **$31.98 million** in face-value debt, affecting 44,000 debtors across the United States.

### The First Anniversary March

The **September 17, 2012 anniversary march** in New York City — marking one year since the Zuccotti Park occupation began — drew an estimated **tens of thousands of participants** (organizer estimates ranged from 15,000 to 50,000; police estimates were lower). The action included marches from multiple points in lower Manhattan converging near the Financial District, a teach-in at Zuccotti Park, and various direct actions targeting financial institutions. Approximately **185 arrests** were made throughout the day.

## Ideological Core

### Critique of Financialization

Beyond traditional anti-capitalism, OWS specifically targeted the separation of financial abstraction from real economic production — the conversion of human labor, housing, education, and health into traded commodities. Graeber's *Debt* provided theoretical grounding: financial instruments (derivatives, collateralized debt obligations, credit default swaps) that had collapsed the global economy in 2008 were not aberrations but extensions of a centuries-old logic of converting social relationships into enforceable obligations.

### Participatory Democracy and the General Assembly

The general assembly model — borrowed from anarchist organizing tradition and adapted by Graeber, the Direct Action Network, and early Zuccotti organizers — became one of OWS's most significant cultural exports. The consensus-based, facilitator-led process (including hand signals: "wiggly fingers" for agreement, "downward pointing fingers" for disagreement, "a raised fist" for a blocking objection) was adopted by social movements in Spain (the 15-M/Indignados movement), Greece, Turkey's Gezi Park protests, and the 2014 Hong Kong Umbrella Movement.

### The 99% Framework

The slogan "We are the 99%" — attributed primarily to Graeber and Occupy working groups — drew on economic research by **Emmanuel Saez and Thomas Piketty** documenting the top 1%'s share of U.S. income growth. By 2011, the top 1% had captured **93% of income gains** in the post-2008 recovery (a figure cited frequently in OWS materials). The slogan successfully unified constituencies (student-debt holders, underwater mortgage holders, unemployed workers, union members) who had otherwise disparate immediate interests, by positioning them all as economically exploited by the same class.

### Structural Critique Over Reformism

Unlike policy-focused advocacy organizations, OWS's intellectual leaders — particularly Graeber, White, and Klein — articulated critiques of the structural logic of representative democracy and financial capitalism, not merely of particular policy failures. This position made the movement difficult to co-opt (it had no specific legislative demand that could be conceded to end the protests) but also difficult to sustain (without concrete goals, maintaining momentum required continuous escalation of direct action).

## Legacy and Significance

### Lasting Political Vocabulary

The "1% vs. 99%" framing became a permanent feature of American political discourse. Senator Bernie Sanders' 2016 presidential campaign adopted the inequality critique as its core message, mobilizing an unprecedented grassroots small-donor fundraising operation. Democratic Socialists of America membership grew from roughly 6,500 in 2016 to over 90,000 by 2021, with many DSA organizers having been radicalized by or involved in OWS. The movement also influenced the political language of the Tea Party's successor formations (which also mobilized class resentment, though in a different ideological direction).

### Horizontal Organizing as Template

The horizontal, leaderless structure pioneered by OWS influenced subsequent American social movements, including **Black Lives Matter** (founded 2013, explicitly modeled on decentralized chapter-based organizing), the **Standing Rock** pipeline protest camps (2016), and the **George Floyd protest wave** of 2020. Academic literature on social movements now treats OWS's organizational innovations as a significant methodological development in the study of decentralized mobilization.

### Fragmentation and Institutionalization

By late 2012, the main occupation phase had largely concluded, but OWS-affiliated projects had institutionalized in various forms: Occupy Sandy, Strike Debt, Occupy the SEC (which submitted extensive public comments on Dodd-Frank implementation), Occupy Homes (which coordinated resistance to foreclosures), and state-level political campaigns by OWS alumni. David Graeber's continued writing and Micah White's subsequent work in Occupy.com and later electoral experiments (including a candidacy for mayor of Nehalem, Oregon in 2014) represented divergent paths from the same movement.

Graeber died in 2020; Lasn and White remain active in activist and media circles. Adbusters continues publishing its magazine and organizing periodic campaigns. The movement's energy dissipated but its intellectual legacy persists in the political language, organizational methods, and moral frameworks of 2020s progressive politics.
