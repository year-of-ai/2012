## Summary

*John Carter* is a 2012 American science-fiction adventure film directed by Andrew Stanton and released by Walt Disney Pictures on March 9, 2012. Adapted from Edgar Rice Burroughs's 1912 novel *A Princess of Mars*, the first book in the long-running Barsoom series, the film follows a Civil War veteran transported to a war-torn Mars. It was Stanton's first live-action feature after directing the Pixar hits *Finding Nemo* and *WALL·E*, and Disney had positioned it as the launch of a potential franchise.

The film became one of the most costly commercial failures in cinema history. Its net production budget was about $263.7 million — with total costs, including marketing and a British tax rebate, variously reported around $306–307 million — while it grossed roughly $284 million worldwide, far short of the sum needed to break even. In March 2012, Disney announced that it expected the film to generate an operating loss of approximately $200 million in the quarter, one of the largest single-film write-downs ever recorded by a studio.

The debacle stood in sharp contrast to 2012's blockbuster successes such as [The Avengers]({{ '/news/arts-culture/the-avengers-2012-film/' | relative_url }}) and [The Dark Knight Rises]({{ '/news/arts-culture/the-dark-knight-rises/' | relative_url }}), and it became a widely studied example of how an expensive, troubled tentpole production could unravel despite a pedigreed director and established source material.

## Background and Production

Burroughs's Barsoom stories had influenced generations of science-fiction and adventure filmmakers, and various attempts to adapt them had circulated in Hollywood for decades. Disney's version was helmed by Andrew Stanton, an Academy Award–winning Pixar director making his live-action debut, working from a screenplay he co-wrote with Mark Andrews and novelist Michael Chabon. The production was expansive and effects-heavy, and reports later described a costly process in which sequences were reworked and reshot in a manner more typical of Pixar's iterative animation pipeline than of a live-action schedule, contributing to a budget that ballooned past a quarter of a billion dollars.

The film starred Taylor Kitsch as the title character, with Lynn Collins as Dejah Thoris and Willem Dafoe among the voice cast of the digitally rendered Martian Tharks.

## Release and Marketing

*John Carter* opened in the United States on March 9, 2012. Analysts and later commentators pointed to a muddled marketing campaign as a significant factor in the film's underperformance: the title had been shortened from the earlier working title *John Carter of Mars*, and the promotional materials struggled to convey the story's premise or distinguish it in a crowded field. The film's domestic opening was weak relative to its enormous cost, and although international audiences contributed the majority of its gross, worldwide receipts of about $284 million could not offset the combined production and marketing outlay.

## Financial Fallout

On March 19–20, 2012, Disney publicly disclosed that it expected *John Carter* to produce an operating loss of about $200 million for the fiscal quarter ending March 31, 2012 — an extraordinary admission that ranked the film among the largest money-losers in Hollywood history. The scale of the loss contributed to executive fallout at the studio, including the subsequent departure of Walt Disney Studios chairman Rich Ross. Later analyses, including a detailed 2014 accounting, put the film's total cost as high as roughly $307 million before a $42.9 million British tax rebate, reinforcing its standing as a benchmark for runaway blockbuster spending.

## Significance

*John Carter* endures as a cautionary case study in the economics and marketing of the modern blockbuster. It illustrated how a large budget, an untested live-action director, an unclear marketing message, and source material unfamiliar to mainstream audiences could combine to produce a catastrophic financial result even when the finished film had defenders among critics and fans. Its failure is frequently cited in analyses of franchise risk, and it acquired a modest reappraisal in later years from viewers who considered it unfairly maligned.

Within the arc of 2012 cinema, the film's collapse offered a counterpoint to the year's franchise triumphs, underscoring that scale and ambition alone did not guarantee success and that Disney's most reliable returns would come from the interconnected-universe strategy embodied by its Marvel acquisition.
